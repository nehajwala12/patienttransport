CreditSwan.AI House View

UK non-emergency patient transport (NEPTS) is a £460–500M, 11–12 million-journey, tender-allocated utility service whose economics are set by three clocks: the five-year contract clock, the April wage clock, and the 2030/2035 fleet clock. The provider side is consolidating fast — EMED now holds the core contract in 15 of England's 42 ICB areas and is roughly three times its nearest pure-play rival; HTG-UK is the only other national independent; NHS ambulance trusts have retreated from the South East entirely — while the buyer side consolidates just as fast (42 ICBs → 26 leadership teams in 2025 → 36 legal entities on 1 April 2026 → a further wave on 1 April 2027).

We disagree with the strong form of the "long-term, inflation-linked infrastructure" framing: rebid risk is real and recent (SCAS lost ~£500M of South East contracts in a single cycle; EMED itself lost Dorset in 2023 and Suffolk in 2026 to HTG-UK), wage inflation has run ahead of any plausible contract uplift for three consecutive Aprils, and provider failure is a routine feature of the market (Coperforma, Docklands, Arriva, SSG, the King's incumbent, Spark/Medi 4, Bristol Ambulance EMS). We agree with the weaker form: the Provider Selection Regime, minimum-turnover thresholds, 5+5 terms, merged commissioners and decarbonisation capex are hardening a scale moat that favours the incumbent with the deepest KPI record and balance sheet.

The fruitful cut is NEPTS, not "patient transportation". That is where the tender pipeline, contract concentration and consolidation targets live. Secure mental-health transport and pathology logistics are the two adjacencies with real pull-through; event medical and demand-responsive transport are not. For a control owner of the #1 platform the question is not whether the sector is attractive, but what wins the 2027–2031 rebid cycle at margin: retention of the 2025 cohort (BOB/Frimley, Sussex, Derbyshire — ~£500M, expiring June 2029 – March 2030), the £465M North West lot (April 2027), the Shropshire extension decision (now), and selective add-ons in secure-MH and distressed regional operators.

Six integrated takeaways

01

The moat is the KPI record, not the vehicle.

Commissioners buy renewal on evidence: BOB/Frimley weighted quality 40% and price 10%; Dorset 60/40; Kent 36/35. The Surrey Heartlands shortfalls of 2024 and the Sussex recovery by October 2025 are the record that prices the 2030 rebids — and PSR Process C only opens to a provider 'satisfying the existing contract'.

02

Buyer and seller are consolidating in step.

42 ICBs became 26 leadership teams in 2025 and 36 entities in April 2026; two merged commissioners now have a single NEPTS provider — EMED in Surrey & Sussex, HTG-UK in Norfolk & Suffolk. Lots are getting larger (NW £58M/yr) and thresholds higher (Sussex: £23M minimum turnover). Both favour scale.

03

The trusts are exiting the South, not the North.

SCAS ceded Sussex, Surrey and Thames Valley; EMAS lost Derbyshire but took Lincolnshire back for ten years; NWAS, YAS, NEAS and WMAS hold the North and West Midlands through PSR direct awards. The private consolidation of 2024–26 was a transfer from the NHS, not between independents.

04

Distress is the deal flow.

Bristol Ambulance EMS (CQC Outstanding) went into administration with assets sold to EMED the same day; Spark/Medi 4 pre-packed in February; the King's incumbent collapsed on six hours' notice. One failure a year on average, each converted by whoever was prepared.

05

Wages outrun uplifts; only scale absorbs the gap.

Labour is ~70% of cost. NLW rose 9.8%, 6.7% and 4.1% in three Aprils with employer NIC to 15%. Fixed envelopes (Sussex £23M/yr; K&M £210.7M/8 yrs) make indexation mechanics the single most important term to verify — and the April 2027 bridge the number to watch.

06

HTG-UK is the competitor to model, not G4S.

Thames Ambulance Service renamed, owned by a social-infrastructure fund since 2021, holding five ICB areas after taking Dorset from E-zec (2023), Cornwall, Norfolk & Waveney and Suffolk from EMED/EEAST (2024–26), and guiding 760K journeys for 2026. It is the only rival with national reach and an infra owner's cost of capital — and the natural bidder for the North West.

Brief correction — Bristol Ambulance EMS

The brief describes Bristol Ambulance EMS as a US holding and the thesis as cross-Atlantic. The public record says otherwise: BAEMS Ltd is a Bristol, England operator (Jacwyn House, St Philips; seven depots at Bristol, Thatcham, Poole, Exeter, Bridgwater and Keynsham) that held the BNSSG patient-transport hub and a critical-care transport and call-handling contract from 2024, carried a CQC Outstanding rating from a January 2026 inspection, went into administration on 22 May 2026 with PKF Francis Clark appointed, and had its operations and assets transferred to EMED Group the same day — roughly 315 staff and 120 ambulances of a 600-strong business. It is an EMED bolt-on, not a second Tiger platform. The thesis is therefore UK-national consolidation, which is the stronger story for this packet — and the first worked example of the distressed-operator lane in §6.

1 · Recommendation

✔ PROCEED — with the add-on programme, narrowly, and with the North West bid decision gated

  • Secure mental-health transport roll-up — five to eight founder-owned SMEs at £2–8M each, folded into Safe Care, funded from facility headroom. Start now; PSR Process C protects acquired call-offs.
  • Distressed regional NEPTS operators — a standing watchlist (§6.5) with pre-agreed administrator terms and a mobilisation team on standby. Event-driven; one a year on average.
  • Pathology / courier bolt-ons — small, non-CQC, same depots; opportunistic.
  • The North West bid — proceed to a bid decision under the pre-registered rules in §9: lot 2 (GM + Glossop) first, lot 1 second, lot 3 only in combination; price-quality posture set by the KPI evidence, not by HTG-UK's expected price.
  • Resolve the two live Southern renewals — Shropshire (24-month option, term ended 30 Sep 2026) and the H&IoW lapse — before spring 2027.

Conditions: four absolute (#01 contract and indexation register · #02 KPI packs · #03 FY25/FY26 accounts by contract · #04 April uplift outcomes) before the next add-on cheque or the NW bid submission — §11.

✘ DECLINE — three things this packet recommends against, in writing

  • The HTG-UK combination at any frame in the next 24 months. A merger with CMA review at a combined share that would trouble every commissioner, undertaken while EMED is bidding the North West and holding £500M of 2025-cohort contracts through their first uplift cycle; and a counterparty whose owner has not signalled an exit. Revisit only on a Real Assets IM process.
  • A HATS process before the NW award. Two simultaneous mobilisations is the TASL 2017 pattern; the failures ledger is explicit about what it costs. HATS is a 2028 conversation, after the NW award and the BOB/F–Sussex KPI records are secure.
  • Diversification into event medical, demand-responsive transport, or the devolved nations. Different buyers, thin margins, closed markets; no read-across to the 2029–30 renewal case that decides the hold.

And one thing not to underwrite: the strong form of the "inflation-linked infrastructure" framing. Until the indexation clauses are read (#01/#04), every April is a commercial event, not a formula.

Q2 expanded — the number the portfolio page does not show

Tiger's public description of EMED gives contracts, colleagues and vehicles. It does not give the figure that prices the hold: contract-level margin after the April uplift. Two contracts with the same annual value are not the same business if one indexes and one does not; two contracts with the same margin are not the same asset if one has a KPI record that supports Process C and one does not. The packet's derived range (£170–210M revenue, 7–12% EBITDA) is a bound, not a number — and the reason it is a bound is that the three files which resolve it (#01, #02, #03) are internal. That is the ask in §11.

Q3 expanded — why the 2029–30 wall matters exactly when it lands

The wall coincides with three other events: the North West lots in their third year (whoever holds them has a mobilisation record to cite), the second ICB merger wave settled for two years (commissioners with the capacity to run competitions again), and the 2030 step to 100% ULEV/ZEV on renewals (a fleet financing decision inside the renewal). A rebid lost in that window is not a £57M-a-year revenue problem; it is a £4–6M EBITDA problem per contract with a fleet lease tail and 300–500 TUPE'd staff transferring to the winner. The defence is cheap and starts now: the KPI record, the uplift file and the commissioner relationship map.

What the arithmetic says

Three levers move contribution more than price does: journeys per vehicle-day (utilisation — the abort rate is the enemy), crew mix (the 73% single-crew share is where margin lives; acuity drift toward stretcher and bariatric work, as Sussex reports, pushes the mix the wrong way unless priced), and the loaded wage rate (which the April clock moves for the provider and the commissioner's uplift may or may not follow). A provider that wins the co-ordination hub can steer low-acuity journeys to taxis and cars, keep its two-crew fleet on the work that needs it, and buy sub-contract capacity at the margin. That is the operating model NHSE's framework invites — and it is why the hub, not the fleet, is the asset.

We do not — and that is the finding. Fourteen contract notices publish a total value and a term; none publishes an indexation formula. §2.8 · Devolved nations and the English exception

Scotland (Scottish Ambulance Service, 400K+ patient transport journeys a year), Wales (WAST NEPTS, ~700K journeys with a large volunteer-car component) and Northern Ireland (NIAS) provide patient transport inside their national ambulance services. Independents appear only as sub-contract capacity — SAS paid ~£0.1M to taxi sub-contractors in FY25 — and there is no commissioning route to a lead-provider contract. England is the only open market, and within England the open market is the Midlands and the South: the North is held by NWAS, YAS and NEAS through PSR direct awards, and the West Midlands by WMAS (~1M journeys a year). The single cross-border exception in EMED's book is Powys, which buys Shropshire NEPTS for its patients at the Robert Jones & Agnes Hunt hospital.

What we recommend against

Modelling volume growth. The national journey count has been flat at 11–12M for a decade of policy documents; outpatient transformation and home dialysis push down while ageing and discharge push up. Underwrite share and yield, not market growth.

Using £38 as a yield. NHSE's average includes taxis, volunteer cars and in-house trusts. An independent lead provider's blended yield is £60–70 (derived); a stretcher journey is a multiple of that.

Treating decarbonisation as a cost line. It is a threshold: the operator that cannot finance 75% ULEV in 2026 does not bid. Model it as a competitor-count reducer, then as capex.

Assuming indexation. Until the clause register is read, every April is an event.

What has changed since the 2021 review — and what has not

Changed. PSR replaced the light-touch Public Contracts Regulations regime (Jan 2024); direct awards became the Northern default; terms lengthened to 5+5 and 5+3 as NHSE advised; thresholds appeared (Sussex £23M); commissioners merged; the trusts left the South. Legal challenge moved from rare to routine.

Not changed. There is still no published national KPI dataset; contracts still publish envelopes without indexation formulae; four contracts were handed back in 2017–18 and the failure rate since is unchanged; commissioners still lack the capacity the review identified — now by design, after the 50% cut. The market is being designed for three or four national providers, and the design is not finished.

What we recommend against

Reading a direct award as a renewal. Process C awards observed in 2025–26 are 6–24 month bridges (GM, SY, B&S, NENC) — placeholders for full processes, not ten-year commitments. Only H&IoW (48 months) and Hillingdon (3 years) were substantive, and H&IoW has lapsed.

Counting the 2029–30 extensions as banked. The +5 options on Surrey, LLR, BOB/F and Sussex are the commissioner's, exercised on the record — and three of those commissioners no longer exist in their contracting form.

Ignoring London. Trust-by-trust, unmapped, and now under a single West and North London ICB that may move to an ICB-wide lot: the largest unpriced opportunity in the South, and HATS's home ground.

Valuation reference points — and their limits

No UK NEPTS transaction in the sources discloses an EBITDA multiple. The two reference points are indirect: Real Assets IM's €200–250M (press) for the whole of HTG in 2021 — ~7,000 staff and 2,800 ambulances, predominantly Spanish concessions — and Grant Avenue's undisclosed purchase of a 400,000-transport US operator in January 2026. Tiger's own EMED price is undisclosed, as was Cairngorm's 2021 entry. The honest statement for an IC is that the sector is priced privately, deal by deal, and the only observable comparables are concession-style Spanish assets sold to an infrastructure buyer — which is itself a data point about how the buyer universe frames NEPTS.

What we recommend against

Modelling G4S as the marginal competitor. It has retained one region for a decade and shown no appetite for others; HTG-UK has won five in three years.

Treating trust exits as permanent. EMAS took Lincolnshire back for ten years after a private failure; a policy turn toward "NHS preferred provider" would make that a template.

Assuming the long tail is harmless. Two SMEs bid for Kent & Medway; ad-hoc spend at a single trust ran to £1.2M a year; every failure hands a commissioner a reason to prefer scale — but also a reason to diversify away from a single national provider.

How do we know the margin band is plausible?

We do not know it; we bound it. The vehicle-day arithmetic (§2.3) gives 8–27% contribution on single-crew work and negative-to-low-teens on two-crew, which at a 73:27 mix and after central costs lands high-single-digit EBITDA for a mobilising national operator. HTG's €200–250M valuation for ~7,000 staff in 2021 is consistent with mid-teens EBITDA on Spanish concessions, which are longer and indexed — not a UK read-across. The right response to an unknown margin is not to model it more precisely but to name the three files that resolve it: the contract register with indexation, the payroll by contract, and the dispatch data that gives journeys per vehicle-day.

§5.8 · Strengths and vulnerabilities — from outside

Strengths on the record

  • Scale and contiguity. 15 ICB areas from the Thames Valley to Nottinghamshire; 60+ bases; the only operator that could mobilise ~£500M of ex-NHS contracts on one day.
  • Commissioner alignment after the mergers. Sole NEPTS provider to NHS Surrey and Sussex and to NHS Thames Valley; the largest provider inside Central East.
  • A stabilisation record. Sussex: six months to business-as-usual, complaints −70%, 85.65% call answer — in the commissioner's own words.
  • Adjacent divisions already exist. Safe Care, Courier and Community Care give the roll-up a home; secure MH is bundled into Surrey and Sussex specifications.
  • Distress conversion proven. BAEMS: assets, staff, depots and a BNSSG contract on the day of administration.
  • Refinanced before sale. Glas Trust facility (Mar 2025) — capacity for add-ons is a question of headroom, not of a new process.

Vulnerabilities on the record

  • Surrey 2024. A nationally reported KPI shortfall on the first ex-SCAS contract; the recovery trajectory is not public.
  • The 2029–30 wall. Four of the five largest contracts expire in 14 months; the +5 options belong to merged commissioners that did not sign them.
  • Two losses to the same rival. Dorset (2023) and Suffolk (2026) to HTG-UK — the duopoly is competitive, not settled.
  • Legacy terms are opaque. Seven E-zec-era areas with no published term; Shropshire at term now; H&W by 2027.
  • Labour exposure. 4,000 colleagues, 470 recently TUPE'd from an NHS trust with pension direction; NLW-anchored pay; April 2027 is the first full-year test of the cohort's uplift clauses.
  • Political visibility. The largest PE-owned NHS transport contractor under a government led by a former Health Secretary who championed "NHS preferred provider".
What we recommend against

Quoting a revenue or EBITDA number for EMED. The packet gives a range and a method; the FY25 filing is weeks away. Quote the method.

Reading the 87-contract count as diversification. Six contracts are ~£75M+/yr of published ACV; the tail is trust-level and secure-MH call-offs. Concentration is by commissioner, and three commissioners now hold most of it.

Treating BAEMS as a template for price. An insolvent asset sale on the day of appointment is a one-off structure; the lane is repeatable, the terms are not.

§7 · The infrastructure thesis — bull, bear and the re-test

Tiger describes EMED as an outsourced NHS contractor on "long-term, inflation-linked contracts" (S9) · tested limb by limb against the contract notices, the wage tape, the failures ledger and the PSR record

The thesis, stated fairly, then tested

Strong form: NEPTS is essential-service infrastructure — regulated, long-dated, inflation-protected, with a demand base (renal, ageing, discharge) that cannot be switched off and a buyer that cannot walk away. Weak form: NEPTS is a labour-services business whose contract structure and regulatory regime are drifting toward infrastructure-like terms, and whose consolidation is rewarding the largest incumbent.

The public record supports the weak form and contradicts two limbs of the strong form: contracts are long but contestable (five-year terms, competitive rebids, two lots lost to the same rival, one national tender halted by litigation), and uplifts are negotiated, not indexed — the published envelopes are fixed sums while the statutory wage floor rose 9.8%, 6.7% and 4.1% in consecutive Aprils. The indexation clause is therefore the first document to read.

§7.2 · Bull and bear

Bear case — the thesis is overstated

  • Rebid attrition is normal. SCAS lost Surrey, Thames Valley and Sussex in one cycle; EMAS lost Derbyshire; HTG lost LLR; E-zec lost Dorset; EMED lost Suffolk. Five-year terms mean a fifth of the book is contested every year.
  • Wages outrun uplifts. Labour ~70% of cost; NLW +9.8%/+6.7%/+4.1%; employer NIC to 15%; NHS Pension direction for TUPE cohorts. Fixed blocks convert inflation into margin loss unless renegotiated — the April 2027 bridge is the first full-year test for the 2025 cohort.
  • Mobilisation is where reputations die. Surrey 2024 KPI failures were reported nationally; Coperforma collapsed in weeks; the King's incumbent gave six hours' notice. One bad go-live prices every rebid.
  • Buyers are shrinking and poorer. ICB running costs −50%; merged commissioners will re-lot; eligibility can be tightened by guidance; NEPTS is <0.5% of NHS spend and easy to squeeze.
  • Political tail. A new government (PM Burnham, July 2026; Health Secretary Yvette Cooper) with an "NHS preferred provider" history; PE ownership of NHS contractors is now tracked by campaign groups; the Health Bill is in play.
  • The duopoly is competitive. HTG-UK has an infra owner, a young fleet and two wins against EMED; the NW is its opportunity to double.
  • Volume drift. Virtual outpatients (43% of journeys are outpatient) and home dialysis erode the swing volume over a decade.

What would prove the bear right

Loss of any 2025-cohort contract at 2030; two consecutive Aprils with uplift below NLW by more than 3 points; a CQC downgrade at a flagship; PSR guidance steering NEPTS to in-house provision; HTG-UK winning two NW lots.

Bull case — the thesis is right in substance

  • PSR incumbency. Direct Award Process C has become the Northern default; a clean KPI record converts a five-year contract into a ten-year one without competition, and merged commissioners have fewer people to run competitions.
  • Thresholds and terms design out the tail. £23M minimum turnover, 5+5 terms, 75%→100% ULEV/ZEV, financial review of low bids: the market is being built for three or four national providers, and EMED is the largest by ~3×.
  • Distress is share. One failure a year on average; a prepared buyer with commissioner trust converts each into contracts and crews on the day (BAEMS).
  • Anchor demand is statutory. Universal renal transport commitment; 75% of users over 65; discharge pressure is a system priority. Sussex is seeing more acuity than forecast, not less volume.
  • Trusts have retreated where the money is. The South East and Midlands are now private markets; the last big in-house lot (North West) is going to competition.
  • Scale wins quality scoring. Quality, integration and access are 55–90% of award weightings; only a national operator can evidence them across every criterion.
  • Two merged commissioners already have one provider. EMED (Surrey & Sussex, Thames Valley) — and the PSR makes that a renewal route.

What would prove the bull right

Direct-award extensions on Surrey/LLR at 2029; NW lot won or shared; uplift clauses that reference NHS cost-uplift factors; secure-MH add-ons closing at low multiples; Shropshire extended without a process.
